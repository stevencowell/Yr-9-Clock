Building an authentic Clock project folio

Your Clock project folio should tell the clear story of how the project was planned, made, checked and evaluated. It is not a scrapbook of unrelated worksheets and photographs. Each piece of evidence should show what you understood, what decision you made, what happened during construction and how the result was judged against the project requirements.

Begin with your planning evidence. Design sketches and drawings communicate the intended form and construction features before practical work begins. The Work Method Statement (WMS) explains the approved sequence of stages and links the work to relevant hazards, controls and evidence checkpoints. The cutting list and material calculations identify the required components, quantities and material needs using information from the approved drawing. Together, these records show that you planned before removing material or beginning construction.

Your photographs should then connect the plan to the actual project. Select clear images from key construction stages rather than including many nearly identical photographs. Each photograph needs a short caption explaining the stage, the work shown and why it matters. Include evidence of important quality checks, approved corrections and progress towards a functional Clock with a hidden drawer. Follow teacher directions when photographing workshop work, and do not interrupt or approach an operating machine to obtain a picture.

A useful evidence process is:

  1. Plan: include the brief, sketches, drawings, WMS, cutting list and material calculations.
  2. Record: photograph key stages and add captions that explain the action, purpose and result.
  3. Check: document accuracy, alignment, surface quality, drawer function and mechanism fit-up using appropriate evidence.
  4. Explain: add sustainability notes about responsible material use, waste reduction and suitable handling of project materials.
  5. Evaluate: complete the PMI and self-reflection using specific evidence from the finished project and construction process.

An authentic folio includes problems as well as successes. If something required correction, explain what you observed, the likely cause, the approved response and the result. Do not rewrite the story to make the process appear perfect. Accurate evidence of checking and improvement demonstrates stronger understanding than unsupported claims such as “everything went well”.

Browser entries may autosave only on the device being used. Autosave is not the same as official submission. Follow your teacher’s instructions for checking, exporting, naming and submitting the completed folio.

Key idea: A strong Clock folio links planning, practical evidence, quality checks and evaluation into one honest, organised account of how the project developed.

Photographing and captioning key Clock construction stages

Photographs in your Clock folio should provide evidence of how the project developed, not simply prove that you were in the workshop. A strong set of images shows important stages, decisions and quality checks in a logical order. Before beginning practical work, identify the stages that will need evidence so that you do not reach the end of the project and discover that an important step was missed.

Your photographs should focus clearly on the Clock and the work being evaluated. Useful evidence may include planning documents, carcass and rebate work, chamfered edges, the drilled face opening, construction of the hidden drawer from suitable off-cuts, surface preparation, each stage of the three-coat oil finish, battery clock mechanism fit-up and the completed project. You do not need photographs of every minor action. Select images that reveal progress, accuracy, workmanship or a decision that affected the result.

  • Photograph the whole component when its overall shape, alignment or assembly matters.
  • Use a closer view when showing a joint, edge, opening, surface condition or drawer fit.
  • Include a measuring or checking device only when it clearly supports the quality check being described.
  • Take another photograph after an approved correction when this helps demonstrate improvement.
  • Check that the image is sharp, well framed and free from unrelated clutter before moving on.

Photography must never interfere with workshop safety. Do not take photographs near operating machinery or distract another person who is completing a practical operation. Machinery must be stopped and the area made safe before evidence is recorded, following teacher direction and the school SOP. Ask permission before moving a project or setting up a photograph. Faces are not required and should be kept out of images to protect privacy. Also avoid capturing student names, personal information or unrelated work without permission.

A caption turns a photograph into assessment evidence. It should name the construction stage, identify what is visible and explain why the image matters. For example, a caption might state that the photograph shows the face opening after drilling and records a check of its position and condition before mechanism fit-up. Another might explain that the hidden drawer was tested for alignment before further surface preparation. This is stronger than writing vague captions such as “working on the Clock” or “finished sanding”.

Arrange photographs in construction order and connect each caption to the project plan, drawing, quality criteria or an approved change. Honest evidence can include faults and corrections, because this demonstrates observation and problem solving. Do not edit images in a way that hides defects or changes the appearance of the work. Your final selection should tell a clear evidence story from planning through construction, finishing, mechanism fit-up and evaluation.

Key idea: Take each photograph for a clear reason, then write a caption that names the stage, explains the decision or quality check, and records the work safely and honestly.

Using PMI to evaluate the finished Clock

A PMI evaluation uses three viewpoints: Plus, Minus and Interesting. It helps you move beyond saying that the finished Clock is simply “good” or “bad”. Your evaluation should use evidence from the completed project to explain what worked, what could be improved and what you discovered during construction. This shows that you can judge your own work honestly and learn from the result.

Begin by testing the Clock against the project brief. Check whether the battery clock mechanism is fitted accurately and functions as intended. Open and close the hidden drawer and consider whether it operates smoothly and suits the design. Inspect the rebate joints, chamfered edges, surface preparation and three-coat oil finish. Also step back and consider the overall craftsmanship, proportion and appearance of the Clock. A feature may look attractive but still have a functional weakness, or it may work well without being as visually refined as you intended.

  • Plus: Identify successful features and explain why they are successful. For example, describe how an accurately fitted mechanism, neat rebate joint or consistent finish improves function or appearance.
  • Minus: Identify a genuine weakness and explain its effect. Avoid vague comments such as “the sanding could be better”. Name the location, describe what can be seen or felt, and connect it to the finished quality.
  • Interesting: Record an observation, unexpected result or lesson that is not simply positive or negative. This might involve how the oil changed the appearance of the timber, how the hidden drawer affected the design, or how a construction decision became more noticeable after assembly.

Each PMI statement should be specific enough that another person could understand the evidence without guessing. Instead of writing, “The finish was good”, explain what made it successful, such as even coverage, a consistent appearance or careful surface preparation. Instead of writing, “The drawer was bad”, explain what happened when it was tested and how this affected its use. Honest criticism does not reduce the quality of your folio; unsupported praise does.

Finish the evaluation by naming one realistic improvement. Choose an improvement that responds directly to evidence from the completed Clock, rather than proposing an entirely different project. Explain what you would change next time and why that change would improve craftsmanship, function or aesthetics. The improvement might involve more careful checking, better preparation, a revised sequence or closer attention to an approved construction stage. Any practical process must still follow teacher direction and the relevant school SOP.

Key idea: A strong PMI evaluation uses specific evidence from the finished Clock to explain what succeeded, what needs improvement, what was learned and what realistic change would produce a better result next time.
